How this is calculated, and why the raw numbers mislead

The problem. NTEP quality ratings are relative within a trial and are not calibrated between trials — different locations, evaluators, seasons and management. In the tall fescue data, Falcon was rated 5.5 in 1987 in the 1982 trial and 4.8 in 1987 in the 1987 trial: same cultivar, same calendar year, a gap of 3.5× the published LSD.

The fix. Each cultivar that appears in more than one trial ties those trials together. Fitting quality = cultivar effect + trial effect by least squares across every such cultivar at once solves for all the trial offsets simultaneously, using every overlap rather than only neighbouring pairs. Subtracting the fitted offsets puts every trial on one scale.

Why not a simple chain. Linking trials one pair at a time only sees adjacent overlaps. In zoysiagrass the 2007–2013 pair shares a single cultivar, which would let one grass dictate that entire correction. The global fit instead brings in Meyer, Zeon and Emerald spanning distant trials, and reports a standard error on every offset so you can see which corrections are trustworthy.

Gain is the ordinary-least-squares slope of corrected quality on calendar year, reported per decade. R² says how straight the trend is; the stair-step view says where it actually moved.

Vintage, not last sighting. A cultivar belongs to the trial it first appeared in. Long-running checks are re-entered in trial after trial — Meyer in every zoysiagrass trial since 1991, Floratam and Raleigh in every St. Augustinegrass trial since 1990 — and dating them by their most recent appearance would misread a 1950s benchmark as a modern release. Checks stay on the reference side of any comparison, which is what they are for.

Two different gain figures. Species gain is the headline: a line fitted through every cultivar in every trial, so it describes the breeding programme as a whole and comes with an R² and a p-value. … as a rate is your own comparison — the gap between the cultivars you picked, divided by the years between their trials. Both are genetic gain in quality units per decade, but the second rests on two groups rather than a fitted trend, so it has no R² and moves a great deal depending on which cultivars you choose. Picking the best of a recent trial against the worst of an old one will produce a large number that is not the species' rate of improvement.

Comparisons. The newest vintage you select from becomes the subject; everything older is pooled into a reference. Gain is the difference in corrected quality over the years between trial midpoints. A reference from a later trial is refused — a cultivar released afterwards cannot be a historical baseline.

Resolution floor. Differences smaller than the published LSD are not statistically distinguishable, however far apart the dots look.

What this measures, and why it is not just the raw score

The trap. A shade plot in Illinois can rate every cultivar higher than a sun plot in Texas, simply because the site is kinder. Averaging raw scores at shade sites and calling it shade tolerance measures the site, not the grass — the same confounding that makes raw cross-trial comparison useless.

The fix. Within each site, every cultivar is expressed as its deviation from that site's own mean. Site quality cancels exactly. A cultivar's tolerance for a condition is then its average deviation at sites carrying that condition, minus its average deviation at ordinary sites in the same trial. Positive means it holds up better under that stress than its own general standing would predict; negative means the stress hits it harder than most.

Read it as relative. This is a genotype-by-environment contrast, not an absolute score. A cultivar can be the most shade-tolerant in the trial and still be poor turf in shade, if the whole species is poor in shade — which is exactly the case for buffalograss. Paired sites are the strongest evidence. Where NTEP ran two plots at the same location differing only in the condition — Dallas full sun against Dallas partial shade, Wichita high against low mowing — site, soil, climate and evaluator are all held constant and only the treatment varies. Those pairings are flagged in the table.

Turfgrass quality is a visual estimate on a 1–9 scale (9 = ideal turf; 6 = generally acceptable), collected by cooperating university evaluators and published by the National Turfgrass Evaluation Program. Ratings are comparable within a trial; between trials they require the correction described above. Quality is never comparable between species. NTEP does not make cultivar recommendations, and neither does this tool.
